Podcast landscape report · window 3 Aug – 3 Nov 2026

AI in Medicine, Pharmacology & Drug Development

What the people building it said on air in the past three months: big themes, where they disagree, who keeps coming up, and the 10 episodes to watch first.
Compiled 3 November 2026

Bottom line

  • The consensus has moved downstream. Nearly every serious operator says AI has made discovery faster and cheaper (Recursion: ~80% fewer compounds and 50–70% less time to candidate), but clinical success is unchanged. Approvals are flat at ~40–50 a year, and ~9 in 10 drugs that enter humans still fail. The money and the arguments are shifting toward trial operations, patient selection and portfolio decisions.
  • Data, not models, is the scarce input. The most technical guests (Leash, Biohub, Arc, DeepMind) agree that public biochemical data is largely "exhaust" and that the bottleneck is measurement. The open question is whether more data plus scale wins, or whether the right kind of data (perturbations, time series, fully measured landscapes) has to come first.
  • Agents are the new unit of work. This shows up as lab-in-the-loop AI scientists (Edison's Robin/Cosmos, Lilly's automated labs, Insilico's "prompt to drug"), agent skill toolkits (NVIDIA BioNeMo) and, most interestingly, evals with a real answer key (Leash Minos).
  • In the clinic, the frontier is evidence and safety engineering, not model IQ. The strongest episodes treat AI like a lab assay: validate it locally, monitor drift, run RCTs. One RCT (RECTIFIER, JAMA) showed an 84% increase in trial enrollment. Scribes save time, but patient-outcome benefit is mostly unproven.
  • Regulation is in motion and thin. FDA created its first Deputy Commissioner for Technology & AI and floated a generative-AI device framework. It also launched an Expedited IND pilot and issued NAM/QSP draft guidances, yet practitioners still call its AI drug-development guidance "thin".

Method. I used Exa's podcast index (3.2M episodes with full transcripts), plus show sites, to find ~45 episodes published 3 Aug–3 Nov 2026. 37 were analysed from transcripts (32 full or near-full, 5 partial) and ~9 more from show notes or highlights only. All are flagged in Appendix A. Timestamps are as heard in the transcript; where only publisher chapter markers existed, they are marked "ch". Figures are as stated by guests and are often self-reported, so treat company metrics as claims, not audited facts. One episode, on AI-designed gene-therapy delivery vectors (Core Memory EP86, Dyno Therapeutics), was excluded and is not analysed.

1. The big themes

Theme 1 — "AI speeds discovery; the clinic eats the gains"

This was the most repeated idea in the window, and it came from insiders rather than critics. Recursion CSO Dave Hallett gave the cleanest split: compound counts and cycle times are sharply down, but the industry has "still not fundamentally solved" Phase 2 attrition. The number-one reason programmes fail is a wrong causal target. Next, ~20–30% fail because the modality can't reach a high enough dose, and then come patient selection and endpoints (Genetics Podcast, ch 06:39–18:20). Valo CEO Brian Alexander says AI's productivity gains are being "washed out" by bigger, longer trials, because better standards of care force harder endpoints (MACE instead of lipids). He sets a falsifiable goal of lifting clinical probability of success from 10% to 20%, which would take ~50 Phase 2/3 programmes to prove (01:42, 32:44). Recursion's Sid Jain (TD Cowen) adds operating numbers: ~60% of protocols need an amendment, ~1 in 5 activated sites never enrols anyone, and most failure is "self-inflicted". Even Dalton's founders, early generative-chemistry pioneers, admit AI is "not having enough of an impact" on industry-wide efficiency yet (10:03).

Theme 2 — Measurement is the moat; public data is exhaust

Leash Bio's Ian Quigley delivered the window's most memorable result. A classifier trained only to guess which chemist made a ChEMBL molecule (1,815 chemists, 60% top-5 accuracy) predicts bioactivity as well as chemical structure does. In his words, models learn "sociology", not physics (Latent Space, 00:30–01:09). In Leash's Kaggle challenge (~4B measurements, ~2,000 teams) no one beat chance on an unseen chemical library (27:32–31:53). Biohub's Alex Rives says today's "virtual cells" don't meet the bar, and that measurement is the most fundamental of three missing ingredients. He places an information-level cell model about 5 years out (Cognitive Revolution, 10:05–19:35). Lilly's Chief AI Officer Thomas Fuchs describes the pharma-side version: protein-design training jumped from 5% of Lilly's internal structures to all of them, and millions of failed molecules are now treated as training signal (08:28).

Theme 3 — Agents and lab-in-the-loop as the operating model

The vocabulary converged on agents, world models, closed loops and verifiable rewards. Isomorphic's Max Jaderberg describes generative design agents scored by predictive "world models", with "half a dozen plus" AlphaFold-class models in-house (07:36–13:19). Edison's Sam Rodriques says Robin found ROCK inhibitors for dry AMD, with positive mouse data, and that Cosmos runs on partner clouds, Pfizer's among them. Zhavoronkov says Insilico orchestrates ~1,200 discovery tasks with frontier models ("prompt to drug"). NVIDIA's BioNeMo Agent Toolkit packages co-folding, binder design and ADMET as agent "skills". The most rigorous piece is Leash's Minos: pre-measured SAR landscapes where an LLM agent gets a 100-molecule budget and is scored against the true optimum. Across ~90,000 rollouts, stronger models leave dead-end chemistry faster (Latent Space follow-up, 01:30–05:06). Rives frames the whole field as "empirical AI": the LLM recipe of pretraining, elicitation and RLVR, but with the verifier in a wet lab (43:08).

Theme 4 — The near-term money is in trial operations

If you ask where AI is paying back now, the answers cluster in clinical operations. Recursion reports 30–60% faster recruitment than benchmark rates using real-world data on 300M+ covered lives. Hallett adds that simulating trial criteria widened the reachable population by ~20% (ch 12:19). The only peer-reviewed RCT in the set is Brigham's RECTIFIER: an LLM reads the chart against each inclusion and exclusion criterion and cites its evidence, with humans confirming. It was validated in NEJM and a JAMA RCT (+84% enrolment, p=0.04), with no bias found by age, sex, race, ethnicity or language (Signal Room, ch 25:38–34:30). Vendors (PhaseV, Biorce, NetraMark) work on protocol design, causal subgroups and adaptive designs. Biorce's best claim is that an FDA-approved protocol was 86% identical to its AI draft.

Theme 5 — Clinical AI: validate it like troponin, engineer the system around it

The clinical episodes converge on one idea: a probabilistic tool is safe only inside a system. MSK's Gregory Goldgof (DeepHeme, live in clinical hematopathology) validates AI like a lab assay, with SOPs, controls, drift monitors and revalidation on every upgrade. He warns that an uncalibrated model score "is not a probability". He also retells the COVID X-ray "99% accuracy" model that was in fact detecting patient age (AI and Healthcare, 12:13–29:38). Eolas' Declan Kelly uses the same troponin analogy and argues for retrieval over a hospital's own policies rather than fine-tuning. Hippocratic AI says it ran 800,000+ test calls with 7,000 clinicians and uses 30 supervisor LLMs per patient-facing agent. Efficiency gains are real (scribes cut documentation time ~50%), but a Kenya cluster-RCT improved notes without showing patient benefit.

Theme 6 — Compute sovereignty, open models and geopolitics

Pharma is bringing compute in-house. Lilly runs open-weight LLMs on its own B300 cluster ("six or eight months behind" the frontier) for cost, security and supply-chain hedging (17:07–26:22). BMS is buying NVIDIA Vera Rubin systems. Isomorphic keeps its models internal. Zhavoronkov says large-scale synthesis is realistic only in China and India, calls US-China decoupling politics "absolute nonsense", and has moved partly to Abu Dhabi. FDA Watch's Brian Malkin notes that early IND work is migrating to China. Kaiser (Making Medicine) argues that fragmented US EHR data is a strategic disadvantage against centralised systems and points to Australia's faster trial starts.

Theme 7 — Regulators are moving, unevenly

Within the window, FDA named its first Deputy Commissioner for Technology & AI and issued a generative-AI device discussion paper. That paper uses a two-axis risk model, flags patient-facing tools as higher risk, and relies on post-market re-benchmarking. FDA also opened an Expedited IND pilot, with applications due 30 Oct 2026, and has draft NAM and QSP-for-first-in-human-dose guidances. Even so, Malkin calls drug-development AI guidance "a little bit thin" and says he hasn't seen AI improve candidate selection. On the EU side, Abridge's Nedoszytko describes the AI Act and GDPR as putting health AI in the top risk tier: a GDPR inspector arrived 3 days after press coverage of his tool.

5. What this implies (builder and investor lens)

Build angles

  • Agent evals with a real answer key. Leash is releasing the Minos environments, transcripts and a preprint, and its Kaggle data sits on Polaris. That makes possible a solo project: an RL or prompting harness for medicinal-chemistry agents, benchmarked against fully measured landscapes. This is where the field is going, and it needs no wet lab.
  • The decision layer. Ahmed's "integration poor" thesis is wide open: an agent that pulls together public trial, regulatory, IP and literature evidence into go/no-go memos for small biotechs. RECTIFIER shows the evidence pattern, which is to cite every claim back to source.
  • Leakage audits as a product. The "name that chemist" trick generalises. Test any bio-ML claim by asking whether a metadata-only model matches it. If it does, the model learned provenance, not biology.
  • A podcast-intelligence pipeline. Exa's transcript index makes this report repeatable. It could be a quarterly scan that tracks claims (dates, numbers, forecasts) and scores them later against outcomes, a personal "forecast ledger" for the field.

Market angles (not investment advice)

  • LLY is the consensus AI-pharma integrator: compute, data, partnerships and GLP-1 cash flow.
  • NVDA sells the picks and shovels: BioNeMo, BMS and Lilly on-prem builds.
  • RXRX and Insilico (HKEX: 3696) are binary-ish clinical bets. Rentosertib's Phase 3 is the field's highest-profile test, with readout years away.
  • Clinical-ops AI is mostly private (Hippocratic, Abridge, PhaseV). Public exposure is via EHR and data names (e.g., TEM) and CRO disruption risk (IQVIA, ICON), the latter flagged by Ephemeral's point about CRO incentives.
  • Basket ETFs: XBI, IBB (biotech beta), ARKG (genomics/AI-bio, high volatility).
  • Signals to watch: Phase 2 readouts of AI-originated assets; FDA's final AI drug-development guidance; whether Biohub/Arc virtual-cell models predict prospective perturbations.

Caveats

  • Several figures are company-reported and unaudited (Hippocratic, Insilico candidate counts that vary by source, NVIDIA's hit-rate claim, Biorce's "236 hours saved").
  • Drug Discovery AI Talk (Dr. Jake Chen) uses an AI-narrated "deep dive" format. I used it only as secondary corroboration (for example, rentosertib's Phase 3 details).
  • Partial transcripts: the a16z/Singhal episode was read only to ~07:00 (later claims unverified); Making Medicine/Kaiser only to ~08:00; Rives to ~52 of 63 min; Quigley ep. 1 to ~46 min of 110.
  • Auto-transcripts garble names. Corrections were applied where obvious (e.g., Exscientia, Razelle Kurzrock, Optum UK).
